## 4. 长难句逐句拆解（7 句）

### 句 1（P1）

**原句**：People with high socio-economic status produce disproportionately high carbon emissions, while also often occupying positions that give them influence over workplaces, organisations, and social networks.

**结构拆解**：
- 主干：*People ... produce ... emissions*（主谓宾）
- 从句/成分：with high socio-economic status 介词短语作后置定语修饰 People；while also often occupying positions ... 为 while + 现在分词伴随/对比状语；that give them influence over ... 为定语从句修饰 positions
- 翻译：高社会经济地位人群排放着不成比例的高碳排，同时往往占据着能让他们影响职场、组织与社交网络的位置。
- 语法点：while+现在分词作伴随状语；定语从句；with 复合后置定语
- 仿写：*People with advanced degrees often exert disproportionate influence over public debate, while also occupying positions that give them access to decision-makers.*（写"精英话语权"主题）

### 句 2（P1）

**原句**：Despite wielding such power, a new study finds that many struggle to see themselves as agents of change.

**结构拆解**：

- 从句/成分：Despite wielding such power 为让步介词短语（Despite + 动名词）；that many struggle to see themselves as agents of change 为宾语从句

- 语法点：Despite + 动名词；see A as B 结构

## 附录 · 原文（BPS Research Digest, 2026-08-05）

People with high socio-economic status produce disproportionately high carbon emissions, while also often occupying positions that give them influence over workplaces, organisations, and social networks. Despite wielding such power, a new study finds that many struggle to see themselves as agents of change. Instead, Catriona Ewart and colleagues report in Environmental Psychology Research, participants tended to locate responsibility for tackling climate change almost exclusively as in the hands of others. This mismatch between perceived responsibility and personal influence, the team argues, may itself be a barrier to action.

To investigate how people view their role in climate action, the researchers invited 38 UK adults earning at least £59,200 a year, or living in a household with an income of at least £100,000, to several online workshops. The sessions used an approach called 'participatory systems mapping', asking participants to build a shared picture of the factors they believed shaped carbon-intensive behaviours and where opportunities for climate action might lie.

Firstly, participants used fictional personas to discuss environmentally damaging patterns of consumption, before reflecting on their own experiences. The researchers used these discussions to build a map showing how different factors interact across five domains — food, travel, consumer goods, energy, and motivation — which participants then refined to identify where interventions could have the greatest impact.

However, although participants recognised the systemic drivers of climate change, they rarely described themselves as part of the solution. Instead, they tended to locate responsibility with governments, businesses, and other institutions, leading the researchers to argue that recognising climate change as a systemic problem doesn't necessarily foster a sense of personal agency.

This pattern also shaped the solutions participants favoured. They readily endorsed structural interventions across transport, food, and consumer goods, but were less supportive of measures that would require substantial changes to their own lifestyles, preferring policies that preserved convenience and personal choice. The researchers argue that this pattern reflects a form of 'climate delay': supporting action in principle, while favouring approaches that minimise disruption to existing ways of living. (It should be noted that some influential climate scientists disagree with the framing of tackling climate change as a responsibility for individuals and their personal consumption; they instead suggest that an individual's effort may best be spent campaigning for government-level climate change initiatives.)

The study involved just 38 relatively affluent participants in the UK, limiting how widely the findings can be generalised. Participants were also classified by the team as having high socio-economic status, but may not have perceived themselves as particularly wealthy or influential, especially in comparison with the ultra-rich. This could have shaped how they understood both their own responsibility for climate action and their place within the systems they identified.

Nevertheless, the researchers argue that interventions targeting high-income groups should move beyond raising awareness of climate change, which participants already demonstrated, and more on helping people recognise their own capacity to influence it. How to strengthen that sense of agency without losing sight of the structural changes needed to reduce emissions remains an important challenge for future climate interventions.
